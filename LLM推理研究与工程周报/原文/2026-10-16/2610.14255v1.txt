Deploying large language model (LLM) inference at scale requires jointly selecting hardware layout, parallelism
strategy, scheduling policy, admission control, and request routing to meet latency and throughput targets.
These choices interact: a routing decision that improves cache locality can concentrate load, and an admission
threshold that triggers only at saturation allows queues to grow deep enough to degrade critical-tier traffic.
Evaluating candidates on GPU clusters is too expensive for iterative exploration, yet existing predictive tools
either ignore batching and queueing dynamics or model only fragments of the pipeline. In this paper, we
present BLIS, a deterministic, CPU-only discrete-event simulator that models the full inference serving pipeline
(admission, routing, scheduling, prefill/decode execution, KV-cache management, and preemption). BLIS pairs
analytical basis functions with learned scaling coefficients and overhead terms, fit once globally, to predict step
latency without per-model or per-GPU profiling. Across 36 experiments spanning six models and three GPU
types, BLIS achieves 6.7% median end-to-end error while running around 200× faster than real execution. In a
configuration search over 420 serving configurations for Llama-2-70B, BLIS identifies a 4-GPU deployment
that meets a 300 ms mean TTFT service-level objective (SLO), whereas competing tools either require double
the GPUs or recommend configurations that fail under load. Two policies evolved entirely in simulation were
validated on real hardware and merged into llm-d, a widely adopted open-source platform for distributed LLM
serving: an admission policy that reduces critical-tier TTFT P90 by up to 82%, and a flow-control policy that
reduces E2E P50 latency by 32–48% on overloaded workloads.

Introduction
Deploying a large language model (LLM) inference platform requires choosing routing policies,
admission behavior, batching settings, and KV-cache reuse strategies under concrete time-to-first-
token (TTFT), inter-token latency (ITL), throughput, and cost constraints [23, 52]. These choices are
coupled. A routing policy that improves prefix-cache hit rates can concentrate load on a subset of
instances; a chunk size that helps one workload can hurt another; an admission policy that sheds load
too late lets queues grow deep enough to degrade the traffic it protects. Teams must predict these
coupled effects before committing GPU capacity or promoting a change to production. Confirming
these tradeoffs reliably means measuring them on a GPU cluster: one workload evaluation (e.g.,
10,000 requests at 10 requests per second (rps) served by Llama-2-70B on NVIDIA H100 [37] nodes)
takes roughly 18 minutes of sustained GPU time, plus provisioning, download, and warm-up. A
meaningful exploration repeats this across hundreds of configurations, policies, and workloads, so
the aggregate cost, not any single run, makes broad exploration impractical.
A simulator that faithfully reproduces these coupled dynamics without requiring GPUs is useful
in two scenarios. The first is configuration search: sweeping hardware budgets and serving-stack
parameters to identify configurations worth deploying on real hardware. Figure 1 illustrates: BLIS
drives a multi-objective search over 1,100 candidate configurations for Llama-2-70B on H100 GPUs,
jointly optimizing throughput and TTFT P99 across three GPU budget tiers (2, 4, and 6–8 GPUs)
with arrival-rate sweeps. The four-tool comparison in §5 uses a 420-configuration subset of this
space with mean TTFT as the shared metric, since not all tools predict tail percentiles. By simulating
per-request queueing and scheduling, BLIS predicts tail-latency percentiles (P90, P99) that closed-
form estimators cannot (validated against ground truth in §4.5). The search reveals that throughput
plateaus well before tail latency degrades, and that the optimal operating point differs by tier.
The second is policy evolution: exploring routing, admission, and batching alternatives before
investing the engineering effort to implement and validate candidates on a GPU cluster. Evaluating
policy variants (an admission curve that sheds load under saturation, a routing weight that rewards

Existing tools occupy different points in an accuracy, cost, and coverage tradeoff, and each

new model or GPU without re-profiling requires a latency model derived from architectural and
hardware specifications, with coefficients fit once, not re-fit per configuration. Second, the stages of
the serving pipeline (admission, batching, queueing) are coupled: each shapes the load the others
see. A tool that models them in isolation, or reasons only about steady-state averages, cannot
reproduce the load-dependent dynamics (§2.2); capturing them at speed without GPUs requires
simulating the pipeline end to end.

(1) Concept. BLIS introduces a latency model combining roofline-inspired [45] basis functions

GPUs, and tensor-parallelism (TP) configurations; new models (dense or mixture-of-experts),
new GPU types and new TP configurations are then predicted without re-profiling. (§3)
(2) System. BLIS realizes a full-pipeline, coupled discrete-event simulation spanning admission,
routing, scheduling, prefill/decode execution, KV-cache management, and preemption, with

Background and Motivation
Selecting and tuning an LLM serving deployment requires evaluating candidates far faster and more
cheaply than real-cluster experiments permit (§1). A simulator can occupy this role only if it satisfies
three properties at once: fidelity, reproducing the latency and throughput operators optimize for;
speed, evaluating a configuration quickly so it can sit inside a search loop; and composability,
accepting substitutions of individual policies (admission, routing, scheduling) without internal
modification. Human operators and automated agents rely on the simulator as a deterministic
fitness function: identical inputs must yield identical predictions, so an outcome change reflects
policy or configuration, not simulation noise (§6). This section establishes the structure of the
modeled system (§2.1), characterizes the coupled dynamics that make its behavior hard to predict
(§2.2), reviews gaps in existing tools (§2.3), and distills the requirements (§2.4).
2.1
The LLM Serving Pipeline
An LLM serving instance generates each response autoregressively in two phases with distinct
performance characteristics. The prefill phase processes the full input prompt in parallel and is
compute-bound; the decode phase then produces output tokens one at a time, and because each step
reads the entire key-value (KV) cache from GPU memory, it is memory-bound [23]. The KV cache
stores the attention state for every token generated so far. It is allocated in fixed-size blocks and
grows with sequence length, so an instance’s finite GPU memory bounds the number of requests it
can serve concurrently [23]. To raise utilization, modern engines use continuous batching [49]. A
step is one model forward pass over the current batch, producing one token for every decoding
request. All requests in a batch share a single forward pass, so per-step latency is governed by its
most expensive operation: a large prefill co-scheduled with many decodes raises the latency of
every request in the step. Chunked prefill, as in Sarathi-Serve [2], splits long prefills into smaller
units interleaved with decode steps to bound this effect. When the KV cache saturates, the engine
preempts running requests and frees their blocks; a preempted request must later recompute or
reload its evicted KV state before it can resume [23].
At cluster scale, a deployment comprises many such instances behind a shared admission and
routing layer. Admission control gates incoming requests, a router selects a target instance for
each admitted request (using policies such as load balancing or prefix-aware placement), and
per-instance scheduling orders the local wait queue. Disaggregated deployments further split
the prefill and decode phases across separate instance pools so that each can be provisioned and
scaled independently [52]. These admission and routing decisions operate on periodic snapshots of
instance state (queue depth, KV-cache utilization, in-flight count) that are stale: by the time one is
acted upon, the state it describes may already have changed, particularly under shifting load.
2.2
Why Modeling Is Hard: Coupled Dynamics
This pipeline is hard to predict because its components are coupled: a perturbation in one stage
propagates through others via load-dependent feedback. We group these into three mechanisms.

Cross-request coupling. Requests in a batch share one forward pass, so a step costs the maximum
over its operations, not their average. One long prefill chunk stalls every decode it shares a step
with, an effect chunked prefill bounds but does not remove [2]. Continuous batching changes this
composition every step, so per-request latency depends on the entire arrival pattern, not isolated
requests. Steady-state analysis, which assumes a fixed or average batch, misses it.
KV-cache cascades. Memory pressure couples requests through a feedback loop: when the KV
cache fills, the engine preempts running requests [23], and recomputing their evicted state on
re-admission adds load that raises pressure further. One large request entering a nearly full instance
can thus cascade into preemptions that degrade many co-located requests; the effect is non-linear
in load, absent below the memory limit and growing with the number of evictions beyond it.
Stale-snapshot feedback. At cluster scale, admission, routing, and flow-control decisions act on
the stale snapshots of §2.1, creating feedback of their own. When several requests arrive within one
snapshot interval, a load-balancing router sends them all to the instance that looks least loaded; the
resulting overload raises its latency, yet the stale snapshot keeps attracting traffic until it refreshes,
and saturation thresholds lag for the same reason. Because these loops interact, a deployment’s
behavior depends on the joint configuration of its policies, not any one alone.
These dynamics determine whether latency targets are met. The default admission controller in
a widely adopted serving platform [31] admits all traffic until cluster saturation reaches its limit,
then sheds sheddable-tier (lower-priority) requests outright; because that threshold fires only once
queues are deep and the KV cache nearly full, critical requests admitted just before it suffer elevated
TTFT from contention with queued sheddable traffic, a cliff that proactive shedding removes (§6).
2.3
Existing Approaches
Existing tools occupy different points in a tradeoff among accuracy, cost, and coverage, and each
rests on assumptions that bound which of the dynamics in §2.2 it can reproduce.
Benchmarks. Tools such as inference-perf [22] and GuideLLM [36] run the real system and report
ground-truth measurements, but they measure rather than predict and exploring an alternative
policy requires redeploying the system under test. They serve as the reference against which
predictive tools are validated.
Closed-form estimators. They predict latency and throughput analytically rather than by simu-
lating execution. llm-optimizer [8] includes an estimator that works from hardware specifications,
requiring no profiling of the target model or GPU. Its estimator reasons about isolated requests
and static bottlenecks at a fixed, steady-state operating point, and ignores continuous batching,
KV-cache pressure, or queueing.
Simulators. They model the system’s execution more directly but differ in both scope and method:
discrete-event simulators (Vidur, LLMServingSim) advance through individual request-arrival and
batch-step events and so reproduce queueing and batch dynamics, whereas steady-state estimators
(AIConfigurator) compute latency analytically at a fixed operating point without unrolling those
events. Vidur [1] simulates a cluster of replicas behind a global request router and predicts per-
operator runtimes with a random-forest model fit per (model, GPU, TP) configuration, so TP
communication is absorbed into the profiled operator times rather than simulated explicitly. Its
replica scheduler follows Sarathi-Serve [2], a vLLM fork whose chunked-prefill scheduling and KV-
cache management differ from current production engines; it does not model admission control or
prefill/decode disaggregation. AIConfigurator [48] profiles individual operators (GEMM, attention,
communication, MoE) per GPU type and precision into a performance database, then composes
the interpolated operator times for a given architecture and tensor-parallel degree to estimate
end-to-end latency; it explicitly models prefill/decode disaggregation. As a steady-state estimator it

2.4
Design Requirements
The dynamics of §2.2 and the gaps in §2.3 together impose four requirements on a simulator
intended to support configuration search and policy evolution.
(1) End-to-end coupled simulation (R1). The simulator must model the full pipeline as
interacting components rather than isolated stages, to represent emergent effects such as
preemption cascades and routing-induced overload (§2.2).
(2) Generalization without per-configuration profiling (R2). It must predict latency for
new (model, GPU, TP) combinations from architectural parameters, without re-running
calibration for every configuration.
(3) Composable policy interfaces (R3). Each policy axis (admission, routing, scheduling)
must be replaceable independently and without modifying the simulator core, so that both
manual sweeps and automated search can substitute candidate policies.
(4) Fast, deterministic execution (R4). A run must advance through simulated events rather
than wall-clock time, so that hours of serving replay in seconds on a CPU, and must produce
identical output for a given seed, so that the simulator can serve as a stable fitness function
for iterative optimization.
Section 3 addresses each requirement: R1 through the coupled simulation pipeline, R2 through the
latency model, R3 through pluggable interfaces, and R4 through CPU-only deterministic execution.

System Design
BLIS is organized so that each of the four requirements of §2.4 is met by a distinct part of the design:
the architecture and execution core (§3.1, R4) grounds the rest; the latency model (§3.2, R2) is the
central novelty, and §3.3 describes how its shared coefficients are fit once and why the pairing
of this model with simulation is the source of BLIS’s accuracy; the simulation pipeline (§3.4, R1)
couples the components end to end; and the policy interfaces (§3.5, R3) make admission, routing,
and scheduling independently replaceable.
3.1
Overview
BLIS models an LLM serving deployment as two layers that mirror the structure of a production
stack (§2.1). The engine layer executes batches on a single instance: it forms batches, manages
the KV cache, and advances token generation. The data plane admits requests and routes them to
instances. Each layer exposes the serving behavior it governs through a policy interface (§3.5), so a
deployment is described by the policies plugged into each layer, not by changes to the simulator.
The implementation and experiments are grounded at the engine layer in vLLM [23], the most
widely adopted open-source single-instance LLM serving engine, and at the data plane in llm-d [31],
an open-source project for distributed LLM serving built on vLLM; both are representative of
production deployments. Figure 2 shows the two layers and the policy interface exposed at each.
The only departure from a real deployment is the engine’s execution step: where a production
instance runs the forward pass on a GPU, BLIS substitutes the latency model of §3.2, otherwise
reproducing the same control flow.
Discrete-event execution. BLIS advances by event rather than by fixed time step. The simulator
maintains a single timeline of scheduled events (request arrival, step completion, snapshot refresh,
preemption) and processes them in timestamp order, advancing the simulated clock directly to
the next event. A snapshot refresh republishes each instance’s load signals (queue depth, KV-cache
utilization, batch size) to the routing and admission layer, which act on these periodically refreshed
snapshots rather than live instance state (§3.4). A run therefore costs work proportional to the
number of events rather than the wall-clock duration simulated, replaying hours of serving in
seconds on a CPU (R4). This is the standard discrete-event simulation (DES) method [7]; our
contribution is applying it to the coupled serving dynamics of §2.2 that a steady-state model misses.

3.2
Latency Model
The latency model predicts the execution time of one batch step and is what lets BLIS generalize to
unprofiled (model, GPU, TP) configurations (R2). Prior simulators obtain step time by profiling,
requiring a calibration run before predicting on a new model, GPU, or parallelism degree (§2.3).
BLIS instead expresses step time as a closed-form function of model architecture (hidden dimension,
layer and head counts, intermediate dimension, expert count, quantization precision), hardware
specifications (peak FLOP/s, HBM bandwidth), and parallelism degree, all read from a model’s
published config.json and the GPU’s datasheet.

cost (𝛽8). Because 𝐿, 𝐵, and 𝑛MoE are dimensionless counts, 𝛽5–𝛽8 carry units of time, so every term
in Equation 2 contributes a time. Three further coefficients (𝛼0–𝛼2) account for per-request CPU
and framework overhead: request ingestion (𝛼0), detokenization (𝛼1), and per-token streaming (𝛼2).
All coefficients are shared and fit once jointly across diverse (model, GPU, TP) configurations by
Bayesian optimization from end-to-end latency traces, with no operator-level profiling (Appendix F).
The decomposition in Equation 2 doubles as a check on whether the fit reflects the underlying
behavior or merely absorbs error. Each phase carries both a compute and a memory term, but by
the roofline only one should bind: prefill is compute-bound and decode memory-bound, so the
prefill-memory (𝛽1𝑏) and decode-compute (𝛽2𝑎) terms should carry no weight. The fit confirms this,
leaving the prefill-compute and decode-memory factors as the only active terms; it thus recovers
the roofline regime rather than spreading weight across all terms, indicating that the physics prior
carries the prediction, and the overhead terms take interpretable values (Appendix F).
Generalization. Because the basis functions depend only on published parameters and the coeffi-
cients are shared, an unprofiled model, GPU, or TP requires no calibration run: BLIS substitutes the
new parameters into the same equations and applies the existing coefficients. We use the full form
by default; §4 evaluates its accuracy across unprofiled models, GPUs, and parallelism degrees.
3.3
Fitting the Model
BLIS’s accuracy comes from pairing the latency model of §3.2, whose coefficients are fit once across
real-system experiments, with the full-pipeline simulation of §3.1, which advances the coupled

dynamics of §2.2 rather than a steady-state average. Existing tools supply one element but not
both (§2.3). Closed-form estimators predict from specifications but do not simulate load-dependent
dynamics, while profiled-operator simulators reproduce those dynamics but require a calibration
run per new model, GPU, or configuration. This subsection describes how the shared coefficients
are obtained.
Training data. The coefficients are fit on 15 ground-truth experiments collected from vLLM
deployments on H100 GPUs, each recording per-request TTFT, end-to-end latency, inter-token
latency, and throughput under a multi-stage workload profile with controlled arrival rates and
token-length distributions. The experiments span six model families (Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-70B,
Llama-4-Scout-17B-16E, Mistral-Nemo-12B, Qwen2.5-7B, Yi-34B), three architectural classes (dense,
grouped-query attention, and interleaved mixture-of-experts with FP8 quantization), and tensor-
parallelism degrees of 1, 2, and 4. This diversity forces the coefficients to generalize: a single fit must
hold for dense models where the MoE terms vanish, MoE models where the interleave overhead
dominates, single-GPU deployments where the TP term is zero, and multi-GPU deployments
where communication cost is significant. No evaluation model (§4) appears in the fitting set
by design. The evaluation models differ in parameter count from their training counterparts (for
example, Llama-2-70B against the training set’s Llama-2-7B), and three families (Mixtral, CodeLlama,
Qwen3) are absent from training entirely, while two GPU types (A100, L40S) are used only in
evaluation. The accuracy reported in §4 therefore reflects out-of-distribution generalization rather
than interpolation within the training regime (§4 enumerates the evaluation models and Appendix G
the full configuration set).

3.4
Simulation Pipeline
The pipeline carries a request from arrival to completion through the layers of §3.1, and its coupling
is what makes BLIS an end-to-end simulation rather than a sequence of independent stages (R1).
Figure 9 (Appendix H) traces this lifecycle and marks the snapshot feedback path from instance
state back to the routing and admission layer that drives the feedback loops of §2.2.
Engine layer. At an instance, an admitted request enters a wait queue ordered by the scheduling
policy (first-come-first-served, priority, or shortest-job-first). Batch formation then admits requests
from the wait queue up to the configured limits on running requests and scheduled tokens, allocating
KV-cache blocks for each. When the KV cache cannot satisfy a new allocation, the policy preempts
running requests to free blocks, selecting victims by arrival order or priority. A preempted request

12
Guhathakurta et al.
must later recompute its evicted KV state before resuming, exactly the cascade described in §2.2.
Step execution calls the latency model of §3.2 on the formed batch, advances the clock by the
predicted step time, and schedules the next step. Long prompts are split across steps by chunked
prefill [2], interleaving a large prefill with ongoing decodes rather than stalling them in one step.
The engine maintains a work-conserving invariant: if the wait queue is non-empty after a step
completes, a subsequent step is always scheduled, so the instance never idles while work is pending.
Data plane. At cluster scale, a request first passes admission control, which may gate or shed it
based on the current router view of cluster load. Admission policies range from admitting all traffic
to saturation-based flow control. An admitted request is then routed by a scorer framework that
combines weighted signals (queue depth, KV-cache utilization, prefix affinity, and load balance)
into a per-instance score, the same composition used by the production llm-d router [31]. These
decisions read periodically refreshed snapshots of instance state (queue depth, KV utilization, batch
size, in-flight count) rather than instantaneous truth, reproducing the staleness-induced feedback of
§2.2. A cluster-level event queue orchestrates the per-instance queues so that this coupling across
instances is simulated rather than approximated.
Workload generation. The pipeline is driven by one of three workload sources. Synthetic work-
loads draw arrivals from a configurable process (Poisson, gamma, Weibull, or constant) with prompt-
and output-length distributions and per-request SLO tiers (critical, standard, sheddable). Trace
replay reissues captured request traces with either their original timing or simulation-computed
timing. Closed-loop session workloads model multi-turn conversations in which each follow-up
arrives a think-time after the previous response completes.
3.5
Pluggable Policy Interfaces
The composability requirement (R3) is met by exposing each policy axis as a single interface that
the simulation core calls but does not implement. Admission decides whether to admit or shed
a request; routing selects a target instance through the weighted scorer composition above; the
instance scheduler orders the wait queue before batch formation; and batch formation governs
the preemption strategy and batch composition. A new policy along any axis is introduced by
implementing the corresponding interface, without changing the kernel or the other modules, so
both manual sweeps and automated search can substitute candidates freely. The routing interface
mirrors the llm-d production abstraction directly: a deployment composes named, weighted scorers
(for example prefix-affinity:2, queue-depth:1, kv-utilization:1) exactly as in the real
system [31], so a policy studied in simulation transfers to production without reimplementation.
Determinism (R4) makes comparing two policies a controlled experiment: with the same workload
and seed, the only difference between runs is the policy under test. This interface structure supports
both the configuration sweeps of §5 and the policy evolution of §6.
4
Accuracy Study
We aim to answer three questions: (1) Does BLIS generalize across models, hardware, and workloads
without per-model or per-GPU profiling runs? (2) Where does it sit on the accuracy-speed tradeoff
relative to existing tools? (3) Under what conditions does it break, and why? We describe the setup
(§4.1), compare tools head-to-head (§4.2), analyze the accuracy-speed tradeoff (§4.3), ablate BLIS’s
modeling choices (§4.4), and characterize failure modes (§4.6).

Tail-Latency Accuracy
By simulating per-request queueing and scheduling, BLIS predicts tail percentiles (P90, P99) that
closed-form estimators cannot, and on which the search objectives of §5 depend. Across the 36

from decode-heavy reasoning experiments (§4.6).

We identify two conditions under which BLIS’s accuracy degrades.
Reasoning workloads. The reasoning workload (input ≈1,030, output ≈1,450 tokens) is BLIS’s
hardest regime (25% median E2E MAPE): small per-step timing differences compound over ∼1,450
decode iterations, so the simulated scheduling order drifts from reality as long decode sequences
hold KV-cache memory and reshape batch-admission timing.

5
Configuration Search
Deploying an LLM requires jointly choosing tensor-parallelism degree, replica count, batch limits,
chunk size, and scheduling, routing, and admission policies: conservative choices over-provision
hardware, aggressive ones violate latency SLOs under load. This space is combinatorially large,
and validating each candidate on hardware means provisioning GPUs and running a sustained
load test, making exhaustive evaluation prohibitively slow. BLIS supports tail-latency objectives
(P90, P99) and arrival-rate sweeps as first-class search dimensions, enabling the multi-objective
exploration shown in Figure 1.
We compare four tools’ ability to search this space for Llama-2-70B on H100 GPUs under a
shared-prefix code-generation workload, and validate their top recommendations on real hardware.
We choose Llama-2-70B because all four tools natively support it, aligning the search space as
closely as possible across tools. We use llm-d [31] as the deployment platform because it exposes
the full parameter surface under test (admission control, routing policy, and vLLM serving con-
figuration) through a unified API. We evaluate BLIS, Vidur, AIConfigurator, and LLM-Optimizer;
LLMServingSim is excluded because it provides no search algorithm and its per-configuration
runtime is too slow for practical search (§4.3). The four tools differ in how they model latency and
what profiling they require, as summarized in Table 1.
All four tools search TP ∈{2, 4, 8} and derive replica counts from the GPU budget; Vidur’s
profiling data does not cover TP=8, restricting its effective search to TP ∈{2, 4}. For parameters a tool
determines internally (batch sizes, concurrency levels, scheduler caps), we use each tool’s own logic
rather than imposing external values. BLIS pairs its simulator with a multi-objective search driven by
a declarative YAML specification; an operator targeting a different workload or SLO changes only the
spec file. The search space spans topology, batching (max_num_seqs, max_num_batched_tokens),
scheduling, routing, admission, and preemption policy (420 configurations, 8 parallel workers). The
search optimizes three objectives (maximize throughput, minimize mean TTFT, minimize GPU

Policy Evolution
Beyond predicting a deployment’s latency, BLIS enables policy evolution: iterating toward better
serving policies by evaluating candidates in simulation before committing GPU time. We demon-
strate this with two case studies, an admission-control and a flow-control policy, each evolved
and evaluated entirely in simulation, validated on a real cluster, and merged upstream into the
open-source serving platform [31].
6.1
Why Policy Evolution Requires BLIS
BLIS models the coupled dynamics of distributed LLM serving and exposes each policy axis
(admission, routing, scheduling) as a pluggable interface mirroring a distributed inference stack: a
deployment composes named, weighted scorers as in llm-d [31], so a policy studied in simulation
transfers smoothly. This makes BLIS a suitable inner loop for a policy-evolution workflow in which
human operators or AI agents explore, evaluate, and refine serving policies.

Simulation-driven policy evolution. Evolving a better policy on real hardware is impractical:
a single workload evaluation on a GPU cluster takes 17–42 minutes (measured on H100 nodes

workloads multiplies this cost. In BLIS the same evaluation completes in seconds.
We drove the evolution with Nous [3], an open-source agentic framework that proposes candidate
implementations, measures their effect, and refines the next candidate from the outcome. Each
candidate admission policy and workload was evaluated with BLIS as the evolution backend. The
workflow applies equally to a human expert; we use an AI agent to exercise the loop.
BLIS’s determinism (§3.5, R4) makes each run a controlled experiment: with the same workload
and seed, the only difference between two runs is the policy under test. The evolution exploited
this by testing each candidate across multiple seeds and workloads, catching failure modes (for
example, excessive shedding at low load) that a single-workload evaluation would miss.
The objective was to reduce critical-tier latency relative to the default policy by at least 30%,
subject to an efficiency constraint (latency reduction per shed request) that discourages shedding
without a corresponding latency benefit. The entire loop, 10 iterations comprising over 60 simulation
runs across agent-designed workloads, completed in approximately 75 minutes of wall-clock time,
of which BLIS consumed 7.5 minutes, the rest in agent reasoning and policy integration.

Validation scale. The evidence in this paper spans two scales. The latency-model training (§3.3)
and the accuracy validation (§4) are single-instance: each experiment fits or measures one vLLM
instance of up to 8 GPUs under tensor parallelism. The configuration search (§5) and both policy
case studies (§6) instead run multi-instance, on real llm-d deployments of up to 8 H100 GPUs across
multiple replicas, with the probabilistic admitter validated on 4×H100 and the soft-reflective ceiling
on 2×H100. The case-study validations measure relative policy improvement rather than absolute
latency, which is the quantity a policy search requires (§6.1), and provide preliminary evidence
that policies discovered in BLIS transfer to multi-instance serving. BLIS’s absolute prediction error
at multi-node scale, where cross-node interconnect and placement effects come into play, remains
to be measured, and we leave this to future work.
Interconnect and parallelism. BLIS reduces communication to a bandwidth-based analytical
term (§3) that omits three effects. Topology is invisible, so peers sharing an NVLink domain,
a rack, or a cross-rack path are modeled identically. Contention is under-modeled, since each
transfer is assumed to see full bandwidth; this is why the all-reduce term under-estimates NVLink
contention at higher tensor-parallel degrees (§4.6) and would diverge further on shared cross-
node links. Latency variability is absent, as a deterministic constant cannot reproduce jitter from
queueing or congestion. A topology- and contention-aware term would close these, with larger
gains across nodes. In terms of parallelism, the main gap is expert parallelism, where the all-reduce
term does not capture all-to-all dispatch and would enter as a separate term (pipeline and hybrid
parallelism [19, 35] are likewise unmodeled).
Serving-technique coverage. BLIS models the subsystems most relevant to policy and config-
uration (scheduling, batching, KV-cache management, admission control), but not multi-adapter
(LoRA) serving, disaggregated prefill/decode [52] with cross-pool KV-cache transfer, or recent algo-
rithmic optimizations. Speculative decoding [26] replaces the one-token-per-step assumption with
a draft-and-verify loop; KV-cache compression shrinks the per-token cache, rescaling the decode
memory-bandwidth term (𝑇dc
kv , §3); LoRA maps to an adapter-aware batch-formation module; and
disaggregation maps to separate prefill and decode pools linked by a transfer term.
Model, hardware, and workload scope. Our evaluation covers dense and mixture-of-experts
transformers on NVIDIA GPUs (H100, A100, L40S), drawn largely from a few popular model
families (Llama, Qwen, Mistral), under ServeGen and Azure traces (§4.1). Within this family BLIS
generalizes to unseen models and GPUs without re-fitting, since the basis functions read their
inputs (FLOPs, memory traffic, layer and head counts) from the HuggingFace config.json and
GPU datasheet (§3.3); a new architecture adds a basis term while existing coefficients stay valid
(Appendix F). Generalization may weaken where those inputs no longer capture execution: state-
space models [17] and RWKV [40] carry recurrent state in place of the KV cache, and multi-head
latent attention [16] reshapes the per-token cache, neither of which the current decode term models.
Non-NVIDIA accelerators (AMD GPUs, TPUs) are untested, and the NVIDIA-fit overhead terms
would likely need re-fitting per vendor. Workloads with extreme characteristics absent from our
traces (e.g., very long multi-turn conversations or bursty order-of-magnitude spikes) may also
exhibit different error profiles.
Platform drift. BLIS mirrors the scheduling semantics of a specific vLLM version (§4.1) and the
distributed inference logic of llm-d, so its modeled physics are tied to those implementations and
must be updated as they evolve. Modular policy interfaces (§3) let components update independently.

Saturated regimes. BLIS’s latency model is calibrated for the region where the system is loaded
but not deeply saturated; under heavy overload, small perturbations in arrival or service times
produce disproportionate queueing effects that no deterministic simulator can predict precisely. The
consequence is bounded: search and policy evolution seek operating points that avoid saturation,
where BLIS reliably separates configurations that meet SLOs from those that do not.

Related Work
Architectural and hardware simulators. Cycle-level simulators such as LPGSim [34] and ACAL-
Sim [28] model GPU microarchitecture, and LLMCompass [51] extends this to accelerator design;
BLIS instead abstracts per-GPU execution with a roofline model [45] and advances through discrete
events [7] where the coupled dynamics of §2.2 arise. Learned predictors such as NeuSight [25]
likewise generalize to unseen GPUs but forecast operator latency in isolation, whereas BLIS embeds
its estimate in a coupled simulation of end-to-end behavior under contention.
LLM-serving simulators and analytical predictors. The tools closest to BLIS predict per-
deployment latency: discrete-event simulators (Vidur [1], LLMServingSim [13, 14]), the steady-state
estimator AIConfigurator [48], and closed-form estimators (llm-optimizer [8], GenZ [6]); §2.3
contrasts their scope with BLIS and §4 their accuracy. Others plan configuration by searching
parallelism [29], composing per-kernel latencies [5], or measuring candidates [24]; a trace-driven
simulator characterizes prefix-cache reuse [38].
Serving and scheduling policies. Recent systems refine individual control axes: preemption
granularity, phase-aware scheduling, and learned routing [11, 12, 27]; energy-aware frequency
control, online/offline co-serving, and network-layer flow scheduling [41, 44, 50]; and cluster
placement across heterogeneous GPUs cast as max-flow [32] or mixed-integer planning [10]. BLIS
forms a substrate for evaluating policies through pluggable interfaces (§3.5) without kernel changes.
9
Conclusion
We presented BLIS, a deterministic, CPU-only discrete-event simulator that predicts the latency
and throughput of an LLM serving deployment without per-configuration profiling. BLIS pairs a
physics-informed latency model, fit once, with a coupled simulation of the full serving pipeline.
Across 36 experiments spanning six models, three GPU types, and four workloads, BLIS predicts
end-to-end latency to a median 6.7% error and runs roughly 200× faster than real execution,
generalizing across model families and GPUs without re-profiling. Driving a configuration search
for Llama-2-70B on H100, BLIS identifies a 4-GPU deployment that holds a 300 ms mean TTFT
SLO under sustained load, and real-cluster runs confirm the validity of this configuration. Beyond
benchmarking, an admission policy evolved in simulation was merged into the open-source llm-d
serving stack, where it reduced critical-tier TTFT P90 by up to 82% under overload. A complementary
flow-control policy, evolved the same way and likewise merged into llm-d, throttles rather than
sheds lower-priority traffic, reducing end-to-end P50 latency by 32–48% on overloaded workloads.
More broadly, by predicting latency on a CPU in seconds and exposing admission, routing, and
scheduling as replaceable interfaces under a deterministic fitness function, BLIS enables broad
configuration search and policy evolution, reducing reliance on cluster-hours benchmarking and
pointing toward serving policies designed by exploration rather than by hand.

Concurrency Derivation for LLM-Optimizer and AIConfigurator
LLM-Optimizer and AIConfigurator predict per-request latency as a function of concurrency (number
of in-flight requests) rather than arrival rate. Production workloads are naturally specified in terms
of user-facing quantities like request rate, not system internals like concurrency; operators set
SLOs on throughput and latency at a given traffic level, not on a fixed number of in-flight requests.
Since our evaluation uses rate-driven workloads to reflect this reality, we derive the appropriate
concurrency input for each tool using throughput-matching, a self-consistent procedure that
requires no ground-truth latency measurements.

Why Re-Profiling Vidur Is Insufficient
A natural question is whether Vidur’s high TTFT error (§4.2) could be resolved by collecting fresh
profiling data. Re-profiling addresses the wrong problem: Vidur’s simulator models a system whose
scheduling semantics, state representation, and execution abstraction are structurally incompatible
with modern vLLM. Fresh timing data fed into the existing simulator still produces predictions for
the wrong system. Table 4 enumerates the gaps; we highlight the three most consequential below.
Monolithic prefill. Vidur models prefill as all-or-nothing (entire prompt in one pass) with a binary
prefill/decode flag. vLLM v1 chunks prefill dynamically via a shared token budget with concurrent
decode requests, requiring partial-progress state that Vidur’s request representation cannot express.
Immutable batches. Vidur’s batches are fixed once formed; vLLM v1 rebuilds the batch at ev-
ery iteration with mid-execution preemption. The preemption cascades and batch-composition
dynamics that determine tail latency under load have no analog in Vidur’s event model.
Incompatible profiling infrastructure. Vidur’s profiling imports from sarathi (a pre-chunked-
prefill fork) and produces data in a schema (monolithic prefill latency, fixed-batch decode latency)
incompatible with the heterogeneous mixed-batch forward passes vLLM v1 executes. Replacing
the profiling target does not help: the predictor expects per-phase latencies for batch sizes that no
longer correspond to how vLLM forms batches.
Resolving these gaps would require rewriting the scheduler event model, request state machine,
batch formation logic, and profiling infrastructure, which constitutes building a new simulator
rather than re-profiling the existing one.

deployed for validation on H100 GPUs.
Search space. Table 8 lists the parameter ranges each tool searched. BLIS explores the widest space
(420 configurations across topology, batching, scheduling, routing, admission, and preemption); the
other tools search subsets determined by their modelling scope.
Deployed configurations. Table 9 reports the vLLM serving parameters deployed on the H100
cluster for each validated configuration in Table 2. All deployments share a common baseline:
vLLM v0.22.1, BF16 precision, gpu_memory_utilization=0.9, paged KV-cache (block size 16),
chunked prefill enabled, and max_model_len=4096. For multi-replica deployments, llm-d routes
requests via its Endpoint Picker Plugin (EPP). Vidur, AIConfigurator, and LLM-Optimizer do not
expose routing configuration, so their deployments use the llm-d default EPP profile: weighted
scoring over queue-depth (weight 2), KV-cache utilization (weight 2), and prefix-cache hit rate
